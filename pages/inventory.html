Inventory & Purchase Orders

Commercial AV integrators were tracking millions of dollars in staged equipment across spreadsheets. I designed our 0→1 Inventory, Purchasing & Receiving suite to connect vendor POs directly to warehouse bins, service vans, and active job schedules, unblocking enterprise pilot deals and driving an 8% lift in platform ARR.

My role

Product Design Lead in team of 3
Led research, systems-thinking UX, and UI, working with our PM and lead engineer.

Impact

  • +8% platform ARR

Connecting warehouse receiving to active job timelines unblocked six-figure enterprise accounts.

The Problem: The Post-PO Blind Spot

In AV integration, an entire job site halts if a single $8,000 piece of equipment is missing on installation morning. Because D-Tools Cloud couldn't show whether delivered hardware was sitting on a warehouse shelf, staged for a job, or loaded on a service van, teams relied on Excel or third-party tools like Jetbuilt.

Goals

  • Connect purchasing, receiving, and staging in one ledger to eliminate double entry
  • Show real-time item status so project managers know what is on order, available, or already claimed by another job
  • Keep receiving fast enough for small teams while supporting serial-number tracking for larger commercial teams
  • Flag low stock automatically on high-turnover parts before installers run out on site

Research Insights

I interviewed 6 operations leads and mapped how they tracked gear outside our product. One power user maintained a 14-tab master spreadsheet with separate sheets per project, reconciliation logs, and low-stock rows highlighted by hand in yellow. It took her two hours every day to update, and counts were still out of date by noon.

  • Time sink: 2–3 hours daily reconciling stock, with 15–20% user-reported count errors
  • Workflow split: Small residential shops needed 10-second bulk check-ins, while commercial integrators needed serial numbers tied to warranties
  • Sales blocker: Sales leadership confirmed 5 massive enterprise potential accounts were frozen until we shipped post-PO staging visibility

Defining Decision: Connected Ledger vs. Simple Stock Table

The fastest engineering path was an isolated stock table where users typed in quantities manually. I pushed back because that would just move their spreadsheet chores inside our app. Instead, we tied inventory directly to purchase orders and project schedules so checking in a box at the loading dock immediately updates the project manager's install calendar.

Legacy spreadsheet workflow

Stock counts lived separately from purchasing and project schedules, so every handoff created another chance for inventory data to fall out of sync.

Third-party tool tax

Paying for separate tools that didn't integrate with D-Tools Cloud like Jetbuilt created duplicate data entry, disconnected project costs, and frequent tracking errors.

Unified inventory platform

A unified system tying POs directly to job schedules. Warehouse teams catch stock shortages early and eliminate manual count reconciliation.

Tradeoffs: Real-Time Sync vs. Shipping on Time

Early in scoping, I proposed real-time updates so that when a dock worker checked in a box of amplifiers, every open browser across the company updated instantly. Our lead engineer walked me through the cost: keeping persistent socket connections open across thousands of tenants would require a major backend rewrite, drop connections on spotty warehouse Wi-Fi, and blow past our 12-week Q4 deadline.

Instead, we updated the UI immediately for the person making the change and polled in the background for everyone else. If two project managers tried to claim the last video switcher at the exact same moment, a conflict dialog showed who got it first and offered a one-click PO request to order a replacement.

Balancing Fast Receiving with Serialization Audits

Residential installers receive dozens of commodity parts (Cat6 spools, HDMI cables, brackets) in minutes; forcing them through serial-number modals would bring their loading dock to a halt. Commercial contractors, on the other hand, need serial numbers and MAC addresses recorded for warranties and compliance audits.

For day-one physical dock operations, receiving clerks worked from desktop terminals at the warehouse intake bench paired with handheld USB or Bluetooth wedge barcode scanners (deferring mobile camera scanning to post-MVP). Scanning a barcode populated the active line item immediately without requiring manual keyboard entry.

I designed a dual-track receiving flow: a fast quantity stepper by default for bulk parts, paired with an optional serial-capture drawer for high-value gear (this path is determined by user settings and permissions):

Shipping on a 12-Week Deadline

To hit our December deadline, we reused existing data tables, slide-over drawers, and status badges from our D-Tools component library. Skipping custom layout work saved two full sprints for the hardest edge cases: partial shipments, damaged returns, and split backorders.

When suppliers delivered incomplete orders, line items received a "Partially Received" status. This kept remaining quantities on backorder while immediately releasing checked-in units to project managers for staging and van assignment.

Validation: Cutting Receiving from 4 Minutes to 28 Seconds

In Round 1, our first receiving prototype forced warehouse staff to scan serial numbers for every single line item. Residential testers took over four minutes to check in an eight-item delivery, immediately rejecting anything that slowed down morning dock receiving.

By Round 3, separating bulk items from serialized gear (though this is always optional) cut median receiving time to 28 seconds, while commercial teams still captured high-value equipment without missing compliance steps.

Impact: Six-Figure Deals and Recovered Warehouse Hours

Operations leads recovered an estimated 10–15 hours a week previously spent on manual stock counts. During pilot rollout, one commercial contractor found over $42,000 in 'missing' equipment sitting untracked on overflow racks, avoiding a duplicate purchase order.

Lessons & Next Steps

In warehouse software, trust is binary. If a technician drives to a job site and the four switches the app said were in stock aren't in the van, they stop using the app and go back to pen and paper. This feature succeeded because every number explained its history: where the item came from, which project had it claimed, and when the replacement was arriving.

What comes next:

Once teams trusted the baseline counts, the next logical steps were mobile barcode scanning in the warehouse and auto-generating reorder POs when stock hits minimums. We held those back from the MVP on purpose to make sure the foundation was rock-solid first.
