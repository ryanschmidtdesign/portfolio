Custom Dashboards & AI Summaries

D-Tools Cloud shipped with a hardcoded dashboard that tried to serve field technicians, project managers, and executive owners with the exact same widgets. Because it answered nobody's operational questions, operators abandoned it for manual Monday morning spreadsheet exports. I redesigned the analytics workspace around modular role-based templates and contextual, verified AI metric summaries—driving 62% multi-dashboard adoption and a 71% sustained increase in weekly platform views.

My role

Product Designer & Design Lead (promoted to Senior Jan 2025; squad of PM and 4 developers)

  • Owned problem definition, field research, and responsive widget layout architecture
  • Partnered with 4 full-stack engineers to define widget schema contracts, query caching, and permission states
  • Led sprint planning, defined technical acceptance criteria, and descoped custom formulas to protect ship dates

Key outcomes

  • 62% multi-dashboard adoption
  • +71% sustained dashboard views

Measurement benchmark: 62% of active integrator accounts actively maintain two or more distinct dashboards—typically separating 7:00 AM field crew dispatch from month-end gross margin and WIP tracking.

At a glance

  • The Problem: A rigid, static overview forced sales, service leads, and controllers to export raw CSVs every Monday to run their own calculations in Excel. Simultaneously, executive leadership pushed for an open-ended conversational AI chatbot that users did not need or trust.
  • What I Built: A modular 12-column widget workspace featuring opinionated starter templates, granular role-based permission boundaries, and on-demand narrative AI summaries strictly bound to the active data payload on screen.
  • The Result: 62% of active users created and maintained multiple operational dashboards. Bypassing manual CSV exports drove a 71% sustained increase in platform views, while giving enterprise sales high-fidelity layouts for live customer demos.

Problem

D-Tools Cloud shipped with a hardcoded dashboard displaying company-wide vanity metrics. For a 10-person residential integrator loading vans at 7:00 AM, high-level pipeline gauges were useless clutter; for a commercial contractor tracking $2M commercial bids, service ticket tallies got in the way. Operations leads bypassed the page entirely, pulling bulk CSV dumps every Monday to calculate WIP and technician billable hours in Excel. Simultaneously, executive stakeholders wanted to leapfrog traditional BI with a conversational AI chat assistant. My challenge was anchoring leadership's AI mandate in verified operator workflows without shipping an unvetted novelty feature.

Core Objectives

  • Provide opinionated role-based templates (Field Dispatch, Sales Pipeline, Job Costing) that resolve daily operational status in under 30 seconds
  • Engineer a flexible 12-column grid allowing operators to resize cards, filter by job phase, and save multiple workspace configurations
  • Constrain AI synthesis strictly to the deterministic JSON payload of active on-screen widgets, eliminating hallucination risks on financial and margin metrics

Research Insights

To understand why teams abandoned existing analytics, I led 10 semi-structured discovery interviews: 6 with active power users (integrator ops managers, project leads, and accounting controllers) and 4 with internal stakeholders across sales engineering and customer success.

Research Findings

  • Finding 1
    Core insight: Users either ignored dashboards entirely or exported raw numbers into Monday Excel sheets.
  • Finding 2
    Workflow friction: Operators needed fast routines: customizing a morning view couldn't feel like building a report from scratch.
  • Finding 3
    Sales constraint: Sales reps needed immediate visual punch for live demos to win enterprise clients.

Design Decisions

  • Decision 1
    Role-based templates: Replaced static layouts with starter templates matching specific daily routines.
  • Decision 2
    Fast card manipulation: Drag-and-drop rearrangement and 1-click AI summaries from active widgets.
  • Decision 3
    Demo-ready views: Pre-populated executive layouts that sales could pull up instantly during discovery calls.

What I got wrong first

Early in wireframing, I pitched three static department layouts: Sales, Service, and Operations. But during user walkthroughs, the approach broke down immediately. A 12-person residential integrator runs completely differently than a 60-person commercial shop; their morning priorities had almost zero overlap. Hardcoded department views would have recreated the exact problem we were trying to fix. That pushed us toward modular templates that managers could reorganize and rename in under two minutes.

Design Approach & Decisions

  • 1. Responsive 12-Column Widget Grid

    Rather than building isolated reporting pages, I designed a modular 12-column grid supporting three widget sizes: a 1x1 counter, 2x2 trend chart, and 3x2 data table. Users could start from a role-specific template or a blank canvas, dragging and configuring cards to match their operational morning scan.

    To preserve page performance under heavy data loads, we enforced strict widget pagination and asynchronous, card-isolated data fetching.

  • 2. Deterministic AI Context Boundary

    Enterprise operators distrust generative AI when job margins and billable hours are on the line. I worked with backend engineering to isolate the LLM's prompt context solely to the structured JSON telemetry returned by the active widgets on the user's canvas. The model was explicitly prohibited from issuing unbounded database queries or extrapolating speculative trends.

    Every bullet in the generated narrative summary includes direct source tags linking back to its parent card, allowing controllers to audit the underlying SQL calculation with a single click.

Defining Decision: Dashboards Before AI

During initial product discovery, executive leadership proposed bypassing traditional dashboards entirely in favor of a conversational AI prompt interface. In usability tests, however, operators froze when faced with a blank chat field. AV integrators are tactical operators—they don't log in to draft natural language prompts; they need immediate situational awareness to identify overdue hardware shipments and technician schedule conflicts before 7:30 AM crew dispatch. Without persistent visual reference points, ungrounded conversational outputs felt opaque and untrustworthy.

I used these testing insights to establish a strict architectural separation of concerns:

  • Visual Grid as Spatial Anchor: Immediate, deterministic scanning of daily operational throughput, technician assignments, and margin warnings.
  • AI as Narrative Synthesizer: On-demand translation of visible card metrics into auditable executive briefing bullets for weekly ownership and monthly billing meetings.

Execution Constraints: Managing Without a Design System

Without an established design system in place, every new visualization card risked turning into a bespoke, unmaintainable React component. To prevent front-end technical debt and protect our delivery milestone, I established a strict foundational token specification: a unified 12-column responsive layout, 16px spatial padding increments, and standardized asynchronous skeleton loaders. Establishing these constraints prevented cumulative layout shifts (CLS) during parallel widget queries and became the direct architectural foundation for the 100+ production components we later scaled and maintained across D-Tools Cloud.

Permissions and speed: Role-based security and caching

The primary systems challenge was data permission boundaries. Field technicians needed to log in at 7:00 AM to review active job stages and van dispatches, but company owners strictly barred installers from viewing company-wide gross margins, labor rates, or executive pipelines. I designed explicit role-scoped permission fallback states for every card, allowing teams to share dashboard templates organization-wide without leaking sensitive cost structures. To maintain sub-400ms page load times across ten simultaneous widget queries, engineering implemented Redis query caching scoped by user role and tenant ID.

Prototyping & Validation

Usability testing: 5-user workflow validation

I prototyped the full flow in Figma and tested it with five target users, walking them through picking a template, rearranging cards, and generating a report.

Iteration: more data density, more control

Integrators rejected early low-density wireframes; operational users don't want airy whitespace, they want dense operational throughput. Testers demanded compact KPI counters (unassigned service calls, overdue milestone alerts, low-stock flags) docked at the top of the canvas above analytical trend charts. Accommodating this density required engineering a compact 1x1 stat card variant and building direct drag-and-drop coordinate reordering. While this expanded our sprint commitment by one cycle, it transformed the dashboard from an occasional reporting page into an essential second-monitor command center.

These validation sessions gave us clear empirical evidence to descope custom calculation formulas from the MVP, keeping engineering focused squarely on layout responsiveness and data reliability.

  • 4/5 users said templates matched how they think about their work
  • All 5 participants customized and saved a new dashboard without prompting

These sessions gave us the confidence to cut complex formula widgets from the initial release and focus solely on template flexibility.

Impact

62% of active users created and saved multiple custom dashboards for distinct workflows. Moving managers off external spreadsheets drove a 71% increase in sustained platform views, and sales began using the customizable views in live demos.

We evaluated cohort retention at 30 and 60 days post-launch rather than measuring vanity discovery clicks. 62% of active accounts actively maintained and switched between multiple operational views. Weekly dashboard interaction frequency increased 71% and remained durable across consecutive quarters.

Users applied an average of 2.4 custom filter rules per card (filtering by job phase, assigned technician, or warehouse location), confirming that teams were shaping the system around their internal organizational hierarchy rather than default presets.

Usage data showed a clear split in daily habits: visual charts and dispatch counters peaked between 6:45 AM and 8:15 AM every weekday as managers routed field crews. Conversely, AI summary triggers remained dormant midweek before surging at month-end and quarter-close, when department leads used the narrative generator to compile owner reports.

Lesson

Executive enthusiasm for AI conversational agents is often decoupled from operational reality. Integrators managing tight crew schedules and thin project margins don't want to converse with their software—they need immediate, low-latency situational awareness. By treating AI as a deterministic, auditable summarizer of active on-screen telemetry rather than an unconstrained chat assistant, we solved a genuine executive reporting friction point without jeopardizing operational trust.

What's Next

Following the production release, our roadmap focuses on automated scheduled PDF/email digests for Monday executive standups and cross-tenant template sharing to allow multi-location commercial integrators to standardize operational KPIs across regional offices.
