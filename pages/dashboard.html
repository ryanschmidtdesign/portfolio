AI-Powered Custom Dashboards

Integrators were completely ignoring our default dashboard because a generic one-size-fits-all view didn't answer anyone's morning questions. I rebuilt the feature around role-based starter templates with grounded AI summaries, driving a 71% increase in beta engagement.

My role

Lead Product Designer & acting PM (5 months)

  • Owned problem definition, research, and design
  • Drove scope, tradeoffs, and delivery alongside 4 engineers
  • Led sprint planning and defined acceptance criteria

Key outcomes

  • +71% dashboard engagement (beta)
  • 62% multi-dashboard users
  • 2 avg. interaction depth

At a glance

  • The Problem: Nobody opened the old static dashboard. Sales, service, and operations all dumped raw data into their own Monday morning spreadsheets instead. Meanwhile, leadership was pushing for a conversational AI interface that users weren't ready to trust.
  • What I Built: Role-based starter templates that teams could customize, rearrange, and save. We kept AI tightly scoped to summarizing visible numbers on the screen rather than trying to replace dashboards with open-ended chat.
  • The Result: Beta engagement jumped 71%, over 60% of users created multiple custom dashboards, and sales started using the customizable views as a core live demo feature.

Problem

D-Tools had a single static dashboard that tried to serve sales, operations, and service teams with the exact same widgets. Because it didn't match anyone's actual daily routine, people barely looked at it. Managers just exported raw data into Excel every Monday morning and built their own charts. At the same time, leadership was excited about an AI-first interface where people would ask questions in a chat box. The real challenge was figuring out how to give teams useful day-to-day tools while introducing AI in a way that felt practical, not gimmicky.

Objectives

  • Give teams starter templates matching their specific role so they stop exporting to Excel
  • Let users rearrange widgets, resize cards, and save multiple views for different routines
  • Keep AI summaries strictly tied to visible numbers on the screen so users can verify every claim

Research Insights

Research Findings

  • Finding 1
    Core insight: Users either ignored dashboards entirely or exported raw numbers into Monday Excel sheets.
  • Finding 2
    Workflow friction: Operators needed fast routines—customizing a morning view couldn't feel like building a report from scratch.
  • Finding 3
    Sales constraint: Sales reps needed immediate visual punch for live demos to win enterprise clients.

Design Decisions

  • Decision 1
    Role-based templates: Replaced static layouts with starter templates matching specific daily routines.
  • Decision 2
    Fast card manipulation: Drag-and-drop rearrangement and 1-click AI summaries from active widgets.
  • Decision 3
    Demo-ready views: Pre-populated executive layouts that sales could pull up instantly during discovery calls.

What I got wrong first

Early in wireframing, I pitched three static department layouts: Sales, Service, and Operations. But during user walkthroughs, the approach broke down immediately. A 12-person residential integrator runs completely differently than a 60-person commercial shop; their morning priorities had almost zero overlap. Hardcoded department views would have recreated the exact problem we were trying to fix. That pushed us toward modular templates that managers could reorganize and rename in under two minutes.

Design Approach & Decisions

  • Scope the feature

    I focused the release around role-based templates that helped users start quickly without locking them into rigid layouts. Users could start from a template, build from a blank dashboard, add widgets, rearrange sections, and choose visualization styles.

    The goal was to make dashboards flexible enough to replace spreadsheet exports without forcing every team into the same view.

  • Build trust with data

    We kept AI strictly tied to verifiable numbers on the screen. The model wasn't allowed to draw speculative conclusions; it could only summarize the visible data on the active dashboard. To prevent hallucinations and keep users confident in the numbers, I worked with engineering to constrain the LLM context window exclusively to the JSON payload of the user's active widgets.

Defining Decision: Dashboards Before AI

Internally, the initial push was to bypass dashboards entirely and build an AI prompt box. But during user sessions, people froze in front of the blank input. Integrators don't think in open-ended prompts; they want to glance at a screen and immediately spot red flags or overdue shipments. Without visual reference points, an AI assistant felt untrustworthy. Once managers could see their numbers, an AI summary that highlighted week-over-week anomalies actually saved them time.

Interviews backed that up, so we landed on a clear split:

  • Dashboards: show current status, surface anomalies, and give operators an anchor
  • AI summaries: pull out key highlights and generate quick updates for stakeholders

Execution Constraints: Managing Without a Design System

Because I was splitting my time as lead designer and acting PM, keeping scope under control was non-negotiable. We also didn't have a shared design system yet. That came directly after this project, when I built and rolled out our 100+ component library across D-Tools Cloud.

Back then, we were working with basic UI kit variants and loose guidelines. That meant I had to sit closely with our front-end engineers every week. Instead of inventing unique card styles or fancy animations, I stuck to a few consistent widget wrappers and standard grid increments. It kept code clean and prevented the dashboard from breaking when users resized their screens.

Prototyping & Validation

I prototyped the full flow in Figma and tested it with five target users, walking them through picking a template, rearranging cards, and generating a report.

Iteration: more data density, more control

Our early prototypes were pretty minimal, but users immediately asked for more data density. They wanted quick-hit KPI counters for the numbers they check every single morning, and they wanted to drag those cards right to the top of the page. Adding that level of customization stretched our timeline a bit, but it made the difference between something operators would actually leave open on a second monitor and another tool they'd ignore.

  • 4/5 users said templates matched how they think about their work
  • All 5 participants customized and saved a new dashboard without prompting

These sessions gave us the confidence to cut complex formula widgets from the initial release and focus solely on template flexibility.

Impact

Dashboards went from an abandoned tab to a regular morning check-in for beta accounts.

They also improved demo effectiveness, giving sales teams immediate, high-impact views tailored to different audiences.

+71% dashboard engagement
62% multi-dashboard users
2 avg. interaction depth

Users reached an average interaction depth of 2 customizations per chart, showing they were actively tailoring widgets to answer specific questions about their projects.

Lesson

It’s easy to get swept up in technology trends, especially when executive teams are excited to ship something with "AI" on the landing page. But our user research gave us clear cover to push back. Integrators didn't want a chatbot; they wanted to know if their morning deliveries were late and whether their service jobs were profitable. By treating AI as a tool that summarizes visible chart data rather than an all-knowing chat assistant, we built something operators could actually trust and use every day.

Next Steps

The team planned to expand AI summarization to other parts of the platform, building on the trust the dashboard work had established. Keeping AI strictly grounded in visible on-screen metrics became our team's standard playbook for future features.
